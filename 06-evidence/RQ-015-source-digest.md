# RQ-015 source digest

## Research question

How should near-A0 learners progress from controlled speaking to real turn-by-turn interaction, including response relevance, turn-taking, clarification/repair and pragmatic appropriateness, without letting AI roleplay hide comprehension or production weaknesses?

## Interaction and L2 development

### SRC-0156 — Loewen & Sato (2018)
State-of-the-art review of interaction in instructed SLA. It synthesizes research on input, negotiation for meaning, output, interlocutor characteristics, task characteristics, feedback and computer-mediated interaction. The review concludes that interaction supports L2 development, but its efficacy depends on multiple learner, interlocutor, task and context variables.

This supports treating interaction as a learning mechanism rather than a decorative “chat mode.”

### SRC-0060 — Lyster & Saito (2010) [existing registry]
Meta-analysis of oral corrective feedback in classroom SLA. Feedback had significant and durable effects, with differences across feedback types and outcome measures. Output-prompting feedback can create opportunities for learner repair rather than simply supplying a corrected form.

Product implication: interactional feedback should sometimes elicit self-repair, but no one feedback type is universally appropriate.

### SRC-0059 — van Batenburg et al. (2018) [existing registry]
Validation work on interactive speaking tasks. Relevant because interactional ability is not fully captured by monologic speaking measures and requires tasks that actually elicit reciprocal behavior.

## Interactional competence as a construct

### SRC-0158 — Galaczi (2014)
Study of paired speaking-test interaction across proficiency levels. It identifies interactional features such as topic development, listener support and turn-taking management as important parts of interactional competence. The participants were above near-A0, so the study informs the construct more than exact beginner sequencing.

### SRC-0159 — Roever & Kasper (2018)
Position/construct paper arguing that speaking assessment should include interactional competence: the ability to organize turns and sequences and co-construct shared understanding. It warns that apparent disfluency or misunderstanding can arise from the interlocutor, so speaking performance is partly co-constructed.

### SRC-0160 — Lam (2018)
Conversation-analytic work on **contingent responding**: linking a response to the previous speaker's contribution. This is especially relevant to Nếp because a contextually relevant next turn is observable evidence that the learner processed the prior turn; a memorized monologue is not.

### SRC-0161 — Lam, Galaczi, Nakatsuhara & May (2023)
Review/analysis of challenges in assessing interactional competence. Key recurring dimensions include turn-taking, repair, topic management, interactive listening/recipiency and keeping interaction progressing. It also cautions that IC rating is context-dependent and can become high-inference.

### SRC-0162 — Youn (2020)
Conversation-analytic role-play study examining proposal sequences at different performance levels. It provides validity evidence that interactional organization—how a learner launches, transitions, responds and jointly manages a sequence—can differentiate performance, while also showing task/context dependence.

### SRC-0163 — May, Nakatsuhara, Lam & Galaczi (2020)
Learning-oriented assessment work that developed interactional-competence feedback tools from examiner/teacher data. It supports giving learners concrete feedback on interactional behaviors rather than reducing interaction to grammar/pronunciation scores.

## Pragmatics

### SRC-0157 — Ren, Li & Lü (2022/2023)
Meta-analysis of 29 primary studies and 54 between-group effect sizes on L2 pragmatics instruction. Pragmatics instruction showed a large positive average effect, with moderators including instructional setting, treatment duration and outcome measure. Written discourse-completion tasks showed larger effects than oral production measures, reinforcing that test format affects the observed outcome.

Pragmatic knowledge therefore deserves explicit teaching/measurement, but Nếp should prefer interactional use over only multiple-choice or written knowledge tests.

### SRC-0168 — House & Kádár (2023)
Position paper arguing that pragmatic speech acts need to be interpreted inside interactional sequences rather than as isolated labels. A request, invitation, refusal, acceptance, apology or disagreement is realized through turns that respond to prior moves and shape what comes next.

Product implication: teach pragmatic functions inside exchanges, not as isolated “polite phrases.”

## AI/voice chatbots as speaking partners

### SRC-0164 — Koç & Savaş (2025; online 2024)
Systematic meta-synthesis of 57 voice-based AI-chatbot studies from 2010–2024. The literature reports benefits for listening/speaking practice, vocabulary, pragmatic/oral communication and affective factors, but common problems include speech-recognition failures, unnatural human–AI interaction and insufficient corrective feedback. The review supports task-based/scaffolded use rather than unstructured chatbot exposure.

### SRC-0165 — Du & Daniel (2024)
Systematic review of 24 studies on AI-powered chatbots for EFL speaking practice. It reports potential benefits for speaking outcomes, confidence, engagement, anxiety and pronunciation, while emphasizing that the evidence base is still developing and design matters.

### SRC-0166 — Saarela, Gunasekara & Kumarage (2026)
Meta-analysis of 51 GenAI language-learning studies / 175 effect sizes. It reports positive aggregate language and affective-cognitive outcomes with high heterogeneity and strong moderator effects. Speaking showed positive gains, but the synthesis is broad across tools, languages and outcomes and does not prove real-human interaction transfer.

### SRC-0167 — Duong & Suppasetseree (2024)
Eight-week quasi-experiment with 30 Vietnamese undergraduates using an AI voice chatbot. Participants improved on speaking measures and reported benefits for fluency/accuracy and a low-pressure environment. The study has a small sample, no strong evidence for near-A0 adults, and does not establish transfer from chatbot interaction to unfamiliar human interlocutors.

## Core evidence boundary

The literature supports:
- interaction as beneficial for L2 development;
- interactional competence as more than monologic fluency;
- response contingency, turn management, listener support, repair and sequence organization as meaningful interactional features;
- pragmatics as teachable;
- AI/chatbots as potentially useful additional practice partners.

The literature does **not** establish:
- one universal near-A0 interaction progression;
- a single valid interaction score;
- that chatbot success transfers automatically to human conversation;
- that an AI system's semantic interpretation proves a human listener would understand the learner;
- exact timing/frequency of simulated breakdowns and repair tasks;
- exact pragmatic syllabus for Vietnamese adults;
- exact balance of formulaic routines versus generative responses;
- a safe automated scoring policy for turn relevance, politeness or pragmatic appropriateness.

Those parameters belong to `EXP-015`.
