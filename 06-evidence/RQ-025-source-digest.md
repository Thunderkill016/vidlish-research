# RQ-025 source digest — corrective feedback and self-repair

**RQ:** Which corrective-feedback type, explicitness, timing, focus and repair opportunity best serve which construct, learner, linguistic feature and task?

**Status:** initial meta-foundation evidence digest  
**Date:** 2026-08-27  
**Registry:** `data/sources-rq025.json`, `data/claims-rq025.json`

## 1. Why RQ-025 is needed

Previous Nếp research already uses feedback everywhere:

- speaking corrections;
- pronunciation feedback;
- listening repair;
- writing feedback;
- vocabulary retrieval feedback;
- AI/ASR feedback;
- interaction repair.

But a method cannot safely say:

```text
wrong → correct immediately
```

without asking:

- what was the target capability?
- can the learner self-repair?
- will correction interrupt a meaning-focused task?
- does the learner need a model or only an error signal?
- is this grammar, lexis, pronunciation, pragmatics or content?
- is the goal immediate task success, noticing, later accuracy, fluency or transfer?

RQ-025 builds the cross-skill policy constraints.

## 2. Corrective feedback works on average

### SRC-0293 — Li (2010)

Meta-analysis of 33 primary studies found a medium overall corrective-feedback effect that was maintained over time.

The important conclusion is not one preferred feedback type.

It is:

```text
no correction ever
```

is not supported as a general adult-L2 teaching doctrine.

Effects varied by feedback implicitness, setting, treatment duration and context.

### SRC-0294 — Lyster & Saito (2010)

Classroom meta-analysis of 15 studies / 827 learners found significant and durable oral CF effects.

Three broad feedback families were effective:
- recasts;
- prompts;
- explicit correction.

Prompts produced larger average effects than recasts, especially on free constructed-response measures.

This supports self-repair opportunities but does not establish prompts as a universal winner.

## 3. Why prompts can help

Prompts signal a problem while withholding some or all of the correct target form.

They can require the learner to retrieve or modify output.

Possible learning sequence:

```text
attempt
→ mismatch/error signal
→ search/retrieve
→ modified output
```

This creates a different processing demand from simply hearing a correct model.

### SRC-0296 — Ammar & Spada (2006)

The comparative study is especially important for Nếp because learner proficiency moderated outcomes.

A prompt only creates useful retrieval if the learner possesses enough knowledge to reconstruct the form.

Therefore:

```text
prompt forever until correct
```

is not a defensible beginner policy.

If the learner cannot repair, feedback should become more informative.

## 4. Recasts are not useless

### SRC-0295 — Miller & Pan (2012)

Meta-analysis found a positive average recast effect but substantial heterogeneity.

Effects varied with:
- treatment;
- grammatical target;
- target language.

A recast can preserve communicative flow:

```text
Learner: I goed yesterday.
Feedback: Oh, you went yesterday?
```

But a recast may also be ambiguous:
- Was the teacher correcting form?
- Confirming meaning?
- Merely continuing conversation?

So recast salience and context matter.

RQ-025 treats recast as one tool in a feedback repertoire, not the default correction for every error.

## 5. Immediate uptake is not acquisition

### SRC-0305 — Shirani (2019)

Recasts and prompts generate different patterns of uptake/repair.

That process information is useful.

But RQ-024 validity still applies:

```text
corrected immediately after feedback
≠
retained independently
```

The post-feedback response should be recorded as a learning/repair event.

The original error must remain in the evidence record.

For acquisition claims, later independent outcomes matter more.

## 6. Timing: immediate often helps, but not always

### SRC-0299 — Li, Ou & Lee (2025/2026)

Research timeline on corrective-feedback timing reports a general tendency for immediate within-task feedback to outperform feedback delayed until after the communicative task.

Plausible mechanisms include:
- the error representation is still active;
- learner can compare error and correction directly;
- new information can be applied again during the same task.

But the literature is not uniform.

No immediate advantage appeared in some studies, depending on:
- explicit pre-instruction;
- laboratory conditions;
- ceiling effects;
- linguistic salience;
- operational definition of delay.

Therefore Nếp should not encode:

```text
error detected
→ interrupt instantly
```

for every task.

A meaning-focused interaction may sometimes justify buffering low-consequence corrections until a natural break.

## 7. Written corrective feedback works, but mostly on accuracy outcomes

### SRC-0297 — Kang & Han (2015)

Meta-analysis of 21 studies found written CF can improve grammatical accuracy in L2 writing.

Effects were moderated by:
- proficiency;
- setting;
- writing genre;
- other implementation differences.

### SRC-0298 — Lim & Renandya (2020)

Another meta-analysis found a moderate overall effect.

Direct feedback had a larger point estimate than indirect feedback, but the difference was not statistically significant.

Learner proficiency emerged as an important moderator.

Important boundary:

```text
better grammatical accuracy
≠
all dimensions of better writing
```

Feedback on content, organization, task fulfillment and pragmatics can be separate constructs.

### SRC-0304 — broad writing-feedback meta-analysis (2024)

Feedback effects depend on whether the target is surface-level or deeper writing outcomes.

This reinforces construct matching:

```text
surface feedback
→ evaluate surface outcomes

deep/content feedback
→ evaluate deep/content outcomes
```

rather than judging all feedback through grammar-error counts.

## 8. Direct versus indirect written feedback

Direct feedback supplies the correct form.

Indirect feedback signals that an error exists but requires learner diagnosis/correction.

Evidence does not establish one universal winner.

### Direct may be useful when:
- learner lacks knowledge to repair;
- error is complex;
- time cost of guessing is high;
- the target model itself is needed.

### Indirect may be useful when:
- learner has enough knowledge to retrieve/edit;
- self-correction is itself a target process;
- forcing retrieval is productive rather than frustrating.

RQ-025 therefore aligns written feedback with the same principle emerging from oral prompts/recasts:

```text
withhold answer when repair is feasible and useful
→ reveal more when it is not
```

## 9. Focused versus unfocused written feedback

### SRC-0302 — Frear & Chiu (2015)

Both focused and unfocused indirect feedback groups improved on subsequent writing in the represented context.

This undercuts simple slogans such as:

```text
always correct every error
```

or:

```text
only ever correct one form
```

The choice depends on:
- learner level;
- current learning target;
- density of errors;
- cognitive cost;
- writing purpose.

## 10. Learner engagement matters

### SRC-0300 — Shen & Chong (2023)

Qualitative synthesis shows engagement with written CF is dynamic and affected by contextual and individual factors.

A correction can fail pedagogically even if it is linguistically correct because the learner may:
- not understand it;
- ignore it;
- misinterpret it;
- revise mechanically without learning;
- feel overloaded;
- fail to transfer it later.

For Nếp, feedback should therefore elicit some evidence of processing when worthwhile:

```text
notice
→ understand
→ attempt repair/revision
→ later use
```

not simply display a red answer.

## 11. What deserves correction?

### SRC-0301 — Brown (2016)

Classroom observational meta-analysis found feedback is distributed across grammatical, lexical and phonological errors, with substantial contextual variation.

Observed frequency is not an optimal curriculum rule.

But it makes one point clear:

```text
error
```

is not one construct.

Nếp eventually needs different feedback policies for:
- meaning failure;
- lexical choice;
- grammatical form/function;
- pronunciation/intelligibility;
- pragmatic appropriateness;
- interactional repair;
- writing content/organization.

## 12. Proposed Nếp feedback escalation principle

For a productive target:

```text
INDEPENDENT ATTEMPT
↓
if correct → continue
↓
if error
  ↓
can learner plausibly self-repair?
  ├─ yes → minimal prompt / error signal
  │         ↓
  │       retry
  │         ↓
  │       if still blocked → more informative cue
  │
  └─ no  → model / explicit or metalinguistic help
            ↓
          supported retry
↓
record original + repair separately
↓
later independent changed-context probe
```

This is a research synthesis, not a validated universal ladder.

## 13. Communication-flow rule

Feedback timing should respect task purpose.

### If the error blocks meaning or task completion
Repair now is usually necessary.

### If it is a current high-value target and immediate comparison is useful
Immediate feedback is a strong candidate.

### If it is low consequence during a meaning-focused interaction
Buffering until a natural break may preserve communication better.

### If correcting everything would overload the learner
Prioritize.

Candidate priority signals:

```text
meaning/task consequence
current target relevance
recurrence
learnability/readiness
future transfer value
```

Exact weights are `EXP-025` questions.

## 14. AI/ASR feedback is still assessment

An LLM can produce a fluent correction and still be wrong, overprescriptive or misaligned with communicative goals.

RQ-024 remains the gate:

```text
AI says error
≠
validated error
```

For automated feedback, record:
- model/provider/version;
- rubric/prompt version where relevant;
- confidence/disagreement;
- feedback type;
- target construct.

Feedback that can materially change learner state should meet a stricter validity standard than optional explanatory coaching.

## 15. What RQ-025 does not establish

No universal Nếp value is established for:

- prompt vs recast ratio;
- number of self-repair attempts;
- time delay before correction;
- which errors to ignore;
- direct vs indirect written feedback by level;
- focused vs unfocused correction density;
- metalinguistic explanation dosage;
- AI confidence cutoff;
- pronunciation correction threshold;
- whether learner preference should alter feedback policy;
- exact feedback behavior under anxiety/high cognitive load.

These remain direct validation questions for `EXP-025` and RQ-026.
