# RQ-016 source digest

## Research question

How should Nếp develop fluency/automaticity across listening, reading, speaking and interaction without confusing faster performance on practiced material with flexible retained use?

## Core construct: fluency is multidimensional

### SRC-0169 — Yan, Lei & Pan (2025)
Meta-analysis of 71 studies on speech-fluency/proficiency relationships. Speed, productivity, breakdown and repair dimensions showed different relationships with proficiency, and moderator effects depended on task/context/measurement. This directly rejects a one-number “faster = more proficient” model.

### SRC-0170 — Suzuki, Kormos & Uchihara (2021)
Meta-analysis of 263 effects from 22 studies linking objective utterance-fluency measures to perceived fluency. Listener judgments were most strongly related to speed and pause frequency, less strongly to pause duration, and weakly to repair. Methodological choices moderated relationships. Perceived fluency therefore cannot stand in for the whole construct.

### SRC-0171 — Gao & Sun (2023)
Meta-analysis of L1–L2 utterance fluency. L1 and L2 fluency correlate, especially for breakdown fluency, showing that personal speaking style contributes to observed L2 fluency. Raw temporal behavior is therefore partly a speaker trait, not pure L2 mastery.

### SRC-0177 — Suzuki & Kormos (2023)
Multidimensional work connecting underlying cognitive fluency to observable utterance fluency. Processing speed and linguistic resources contribute differently to speed, breakdown and repair dimensions. This supports measuring underlying retrieval/encoding efficiency separately from surface speech timing.

## Practice, proceduralization and transfer

### SRC-0172 — de Jong & Perfetti (2011)
Experimental ESL fluency training using repeated 4/3/2-minute speech. Both groups improved during training, but retained posttest gains were found particularly where learners repeated the same topic; the authors interpret evidence in terms of proceduralization of linguistic knowledge. Repeated-topic fluency improvement is useful but must still be tested on transfer tasks.

### SRC-0173 — DeKeyser (1997)
Skill-acquisition study showing gradual automatization of explicitly learned morphosyntax through systematic comprehension/production practice, with strong skill specificity. Practice in one modality/operation should therefore not automatically be counted as automaticity in another.

### SRC-0175 — Suzuki (2021)
Study comparing blocked and interleaved oral task repetition. Practice schedule changed fluency development and transfer to new tasks. Some benefits of concentrated repetition can transfer, but not all training advantages generalize.

### SRC-0176 — Suzuki & Hanzawa (2022)
Repeated oral narrative practice under massed/short-spaced/long-spaced schedules. Massed practice produced a trade-off: some breakdown-fluency gains but worse speed/repair characteristics on an immediate novel-task test, and limited schedule differences after delay. This is strong evidence against optimizing one temporal metric in isolation.

### SRC-0174 — Kakitani & Kormos (2024)
Randomized fluency training with short- versus long-spaced practice. Both experimental spacing schedules showed similar gains at 7- and 28-day posttests. The study supports distributed fluency practice but does not justify one universal spacing interval.

### SRC-0058 — Task repetition and L2 oral performance meta-analysis [existing registry]
The research base broadly supports task repetition as a means of changing oral performance, but effects vary by outcome, repetition design and task characteristics. Same-task performance and novel-task transfer must remain distinct outcomes.

## Underlying automatic processing

### SRC-0178 — Hanzawa & Suzuki (2023)
Six-month EFL study of lexical/grammar processing automaticity and utterance-fluency development. Faster/more stable grammar processing predicted some reductions in mid-clause pausing. It provides direct evidence that underlying processing efficiency and observable speech fluency are related but not identical.

### SRC-0173 — DeKeyser (1997), reused
Automaticity is practice- and skill-specific. Accuracy alone is insufficient; latency/processing efficiency needs converging evidence, while faster but error-prone responding is not successful automatization.

## Formulaic language / chunks

### SRC-0179 — Li & Lei (2024)
Meta-analysis of collocation instruction. Collocation instruction was effective overall, with instructional method moderating outcomes. This supports deliberately strengthening high-value multiword language, though the meta-analysis targets collocation learning rather than proving generalized speech fluency.

### SRC-0180 — Tavakoli & Uchihara (2020)
Across assessed proficiency levels, multiword-sequence use was associated with several oral-fluency dimensions, including articulation rate and pausing. Lower-proficiency speakers could reproduce task-specific chunks verbatim while higher-proficiency speakers used them more flexibly, reinforcing the need to test flexible reuse rather than chunk recitation.

### SRC-0061 — formulaic sequence evidence [existing registry]
Existing Nếp research already treats chunks as meaningful language units. RQ-016 extends this by requiring fast retrieval to be demonstrated in changed contexts, not just memorized phrase playback.

## Reading fluency evidence already in RQ-013

RQ-013 established that:
- passage fluency matters;
- repeated reading can increase rate;
- audio-assisted/repeated performance can improve fluency measures;
- practiced-text rate is not independent unseen-text reading;
- comprehension must remain paired with rate/efficiency.

Relevant existing evidence includes `SRC-0141`, `SRC-0142`, `SRC-0143` and `CLM-READ-011`–`CLM-READ-015`.

This same evidence rule should generalize across modalities:

```text
faster on practiced item
≠
fluent independent use
```

## Spacing evidence already in RQ-004

Existing retrieval/spacing research (`SRC-0003` and RQ-004) supports distributed retrieval for retention but does not provide one universal interval. Fluency practice likewise needs empirical scheduling rather than a fixed “repeat N times” rule.

## Critical distinctions for Nếp

### Performance vs learning

```text
repeated task gets faster
```

can reflect:
- content familiarity;
- lexical/syntactic priming;
- reduced conceptualization demand;
- memorized wording;
- genuine proceduralization;
- some combination.

Only changed-task/delayed evidence can distinguish broader learning from local performance gain.

### Speed vs automaticity

Automaticity is not simply low reaction time. Stronger evidence requires some combination of:
- maintained accuracy/meaning;
- faster processing;
- more stable processing;
- lower breakdown cost;
- successful transfer;
- retention after delay.

### Repair is not automatically disfluency

Repair fluency has weaker/less stable relationships with proficiency than speed and breakdown in meta-analytic work. In interaction, repair can also be a competent conversational strategy (RQ-015). Nếp must distinguish:
- self-repair caused by unstable formulation;
- useful monitoring/correction;
- interactional clarification/repair.

## Evidence gap

The literature does not establish one cross-modal fluency algorithm for Vietnamese adults near A0.

Still unresolved:
- when an item is ready for fluency practice rather than initial learning;
- optimal same-item repetition count;
- when to switch from blocked repetition to varied/new contexts;
- exact spacing schedule;
- useful time-pressure policy, if any;
- acceptable speed–accuracy–meaning trade-off;
- which speech temporal measures are robust enough on near-A0 short responses;
- useful reading-rate measures for very short beginner text;
- how to measure listening processing fluency without turning comprehension into reaction-time gaming;
- how to account for L1/personal speaking style;
- when formulaic chunks are flexibly available rather than memorized scripts;
- how automaticity should influence mastery/review decisions.

Those parameters belong to `EXP-016`.
