# RQ-009 source digest — authentic video readiness

## Question

What vocabulary coverage and speech/video characteristics make a short authentic English clip usable for a learner, especially before Nếp makes authentic video a regular learning source?

## High-signal sources

### SRC-0087 — van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013)

Direct manipulation of lexical coverage in informal L2 listening. Comprehension improved with coverage. Around 95% produced relatively good comprehension with less between-learner variation than 90%; 98% was better when very high comprehension was desired. The study does **not** establish a universal video threshold.

### SRC-0088 — Webb (2021)

Critical review of lexical coverage research. The main warning is important for Nếp: commonly quoted coverage figures are based on a small number of direct studies, and comprehension varies by learner, text, modality and task. Coverage is a probabilistic predictor, not a guarantee.

### SRC-0089 — Durbahn, Rodgers & Peters (2020)

Viewing-comprehension study with a 30-minute documentary. Lexical coverage was positively related to viewing comprehension, especially for audio-based questions; imagery changed the role vocabulary played. This supports treating viewing as more than listening with pictures added.

### SRC-0090 — Durbahn, Rodgers, Macis & Peters (2024)

Direct experimental manipulation of lexical coverage in four short documentaries. Comprehension fell as coverage fell. The authors proposed approximately 95% as an optimal-adequate level and 80% as a minimal-adequate level **for their short-documentary viewing task**. Nếp must not generalize 80% into an audio-learning threshold.

### SRC-0091 / SRC-0092 — Webb & Rodgers (2009)

Large corpus analyses of TV programs and movie scripts. Typical 95% coverage was around the most frequent 3,000 word families plus proper nouns/marginal words, while 98% required much more vocabulary; genre and episode variation was substantial. These are corpus-demand estimates, not learner-readiness rules.

### SRC-0093 — Ha (2022)

A methodological update using very large TV/movie/soap corpora and BNC/COCA lists. Approximately 3,000 and 5,000 frequency levels covered about 95% and 98% of scripted informal dialogue overall. Again, clip-level variation remains more important for an adaptive product than a single global vocabulary-size gate.

### SRC-0094 — Pujadas & Muñoz (2020)

Longitudinal TV-viewing study with beginner adolescent EFL learners. Prior vocabulary knowledge and lexical coverage predicted comprehension; L1 subtitles improved content comprehension relative to captions in this setting. This demonstrates that “understood the episode” can be achieved through support without proving independent listening.

### SRC-0095 — Peters (2019)

Imagery and on-screen text affected vocabulary learning from audiovisual input. Words whose referents appeared close in time to their spoken occurrence were more likely to be learned. Visual-semantic grounding is therefore a useful clip property, not decorative metadata.

### SRC-0096 — Montero Perez (2022)

State-of-the-art review of audiovisual input and on-screen text. Effects depend on learner proficiency, vocabulary demands, content characteristics and on-screen text. The review reinforces the need for graded audiovisual input rather than a one-size-fits-all “authentic video"�� mode.

### SRC-0097 — Mahalingappa, Zong & Polat (2024)

Captioning and playback speed interacted with comprehension and learner proficiency in a 287-participant study. Slower playback with captions was especially supportive for easier comprehension questions, while proficiency/listening ability moderated effects. Speech rate is therefore a learner-relative load factor rather than a universal WPM cutoff.

## Evidence boundary

The literature supports these broad conclusions:

1. higher lexical coverage generally improves listening/viewing comprehension;
2. imagery can lower the lexical burden of understanding audiovisual messages;
3. coverage thresholds depend on modality, material, learner and comprehension criterion;
4. corpus-wide vocabulary-size figures cannot determine whether one specific clip is usable;
5. speech rate, support, topic/context and learner proficiency modify difficulty;
6. content comprehension with captions/subtitles or strong imagery is not the same construct as independent audio comprehension.

The literature does **not** establish:

- one vocabulary count at which Vietnamese near-A0 learners should unlock authentic video;
- one universal lexical-coverage threshold for every video genre;
- one universal words-per-minute threshold;
- that a clip understood with subtitles is ready for listening practice;
- that full-video difficulty can be inferred from one average metric.
